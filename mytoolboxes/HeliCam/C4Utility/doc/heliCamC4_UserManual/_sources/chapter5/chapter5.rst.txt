.. _D:

D) Advanced Concepts and Control
================================

This chapter offers a detailed, mathematical, and mechanistic view of
the measured signal, which enables the user to exploit
the full potential of the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 lock-in camera.

Lock-In Amplification
---------------------

This first section aims to convey the basic theory behind lock-in
amplification in depth to users with a technical background, who are not
fully familiar with the subject. Later sections draw heavily on the
picture presented here.

A lock-in amplifier exclusively extracts frequency components in a
defined band around a tunable reference frequency from the input.
Lock-in amplifiers can be used to isolate the information-bearing
modulation amplitude from a known carrier wave in a process called
demodulation. Due to their selectivity for specific frequencies, lock-in
detectors are effective at suppressing background noise.

The implementation of lock-in amplification comprises two key steps: 

   - First, the input signal is compared to a standard oscillation called
     reference signal by multiplication. This process is also called
     heterodyning - or homodyning if the reference oscillation has the same
     frequency as the input.
   - Then, the result is averaged in time.
   
Lock-in amplification is 
equivalently described in the frequency domain as (down-)mixing 
and subsequent low-pass filtering.

Single- and Dual-Phase
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

In their most basic form, so-called *single-phase* lock-in
amplifiers also attenuate signal components that are out of phase with
the reference signal even if they oscillate at the same frequency.
*Dual-phase* or *phase-sensitive* lock-in amplification refers to a more
advanced technique, which can detect both the amplitude of the input
oscillation and its phase shift with respect to the reference signal.
With this approach, used by the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4, the lock-in
process is repeated once more with the reference signal shifted by
:math:`\frac{\pi}{2}` (see :numref:`DP_LIA`).

.. figure:: img/dual_phase_lock_in_amplification.png
      :name: DP_LIA
      :align: center
      :width: 100%

      Block Diagram of Single- and Dual-phase Lock-In Amplification

      Lock-in amplification selectively extracts frequency components around the
      reference frequency. To this end, the input is mixed with a reference
      signal provided by a local oscillator and subsequently low-pass
      filtered. Single-phase lock-in amplification (black part) not only
      suppresses signal components with frequency- but also with
      phase-mismatch relative to the reference signal. With a dual-phase
      lock-in design (grey part included), the amplification is repeated with
      a :math:`\frac{\pi}{2}`- shifted copy of the reference signal. The two
      outputs components, “In-Phase” and “Quadrature” after their phase
      relation to the reference, are usually transformed to polar coordinates.
      In this manner, both the signal amplitude of the passing frequency
      components and their phase with respect to the local oscillator can be
      retrieved. In the literature on lock-in amplification, “In-Phase” and
      “Quadrature” components are often referred to as X and Y, respectively.

Most lock-in amplifiers use a sine wave as reference signal. It is a
pulse wave in some instruments, however, including the
heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4. These lock-in amplifiers are also sensitive to
odd harmonics of the fundamental reference frequency. For the sake of
simplicity, we will first focus on ideal dual-phase demodulation with a
perfect sine and its :math:`\frac{\pi}{2}`-shifted twin, a cosine wave,
as reference signals. Only then will we consider the impact of
demodulating with a pulse wave instead in the next section.

.. _Idealized_Signal_Processing:

Idealized Signal Processing
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

A mathematical examination of lock-in amplification reveals how the
processing steps outlined above act upon the input. We will set out in
time domain to track the treatment of the signal directly as it is
implemented by the hardware. The analysis of the lock-in principle in
the frequency domain offers a complementary view. Although very
intuitive, this second perspective requires the reader to be familiar
with the Fourier transform.

Time Domain
^^^^^^^^^^^

In time domain, signals are expressed by their value as a function of
time. Not with all inputs is lock-in amplification easily described
using this representation.

Sinusoid Input
''''''''''''''

Oscillatory inputs with a single frequency arise often, are suitable for
analysis in time domain and the results generalize readily. They can
also be considered as pure carriers, i.e., amplitude modulated signals
of the simplest form, which do not contain any information. Let us
initially assume a sinusoid input signal :math:`s_{in}` with constant
amplitude, :math:`A_{in}`, frequency, :math:`f_{in}`, and phase, 
:math:`{\varphi}_{in}`.

.. math::
     :label: s_in_ideal

     s_{in}(t) = A_{in} \cdot cos\left( 2{\pi f}_{in}t + \varphi_{in} \right)

Dual-phase lock-in amplification of this input, as described formally below,
is graphically summarized in :numref:`LIA_monofreq` at the end of the paragraph.
Using complex numbers, this process can be expressed 
concisely. Euler's formula :eq:`euler` establishes a relationship between 
the trigonometric functions and the complex exponential function.

.. math::
     :label: euler

     e^{ix} = \cos(x) + i\sin(x)

From :eq:`euler` follows that the cosine can be written as a sum of complex
exponentials.

.. math::
     :label: cos_euler

     cos(x) = \frac{e^{ix} + e^{- ix}}{2}

Using this identity :eq:`euler`, the input signal :eq:`s_in_ideal` can be recast in complex
form a clockwise and a counterclockwise rotating component in the
complex plane.

.. math::
     :label: s_in_complex

     s_{in}(t) = A_{in}\frac{e^{i\left( 2{\pi f}_{in}t + \varphi_{in} \right)} +
     e^{- i\left( 2{\pi f}_{in}t + \varphi_{in} \right)}}{2}


Comparison to Reference Signal
''''''''''''''''''''''''''''''

Writing the input in this fashion, the two-stage processing of the input
in dual-phase demodulation can be mathematically treated in a single pass with
a single, yet complex, reference signal :math:`s_{ref}(t)`. The real
part of :math:`s_{ref}` is in phase with a local oscillator, that is
setting the pace. Its imaginary part accounts for the second
demodulation and is thus shifted by :math:`\frac{\pi}{2}` with respect
to the real part. Here, :math:`s_{ref}`\ ’s two parts are presumed
sinusoid. Euler’s formula :eq:`euler` is used once more for the second equality.

.. math::
     :label: s_ref_ideal

     s_{ref}(t) = 2\cos\left( 2 \pi f_{ref}t \right) - i2\sin\left( 2 \pi f_{ref}t \right) 
     = 2e^{- i\left( 2 \pi f_{ref} t \right)}


Mixing the input signal :eq:`cos_euler` with the reference signal :eq:`s_ref_ideal` yields:

.. math::
     :label: s_mix_ideal

     s_{mix}(t) = s_{in}(t) \cdot s_{ref}(t) = A_{in}\left( e^{i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} - f}_{ref})t + 
     \varphi_{in} \right)} + e^{- i\left( 2 {\pi(f_{in} + f}_{ref})t + \varphi_{in} \right)} \right)


To measure the magnitude of the input signal, the frequency of the
configurable reference signal, :math:`f_{ref}`, is closely matched to
the input’s frequency :math:`f_{in}`. The first step of lock-in
amplification therefore results in two complex sinusoids with very
different oscillation frequencies, called heterodynes.

     - The higher frequency is given by the sum of the signal and the reference frequency and thus commonly referred to as the :math:`2f`-*component*.
     - The lower, so-called *baseband* frequency, is given by the difference between the two.

Only the baseband is of interest in lock-in amplification.

Averaging in Time
'''''''''''''''''

The mixed signal :math:`s_{mix}(t)` is then averaged over a shifting time interval to produce
the lock-in output. Intuitively, computing this moving average on a sine
wave that is much longer than the sine’s
period, reduces the oscillation amplitude. Conversely, if this “mean” is
taken over a very short time compared to the sine’s period, its
amplitude is largely unaffected. Oscillations with higher frequencies
therefore tend to be selectively more attenuated by the averaging.

The moving average is a linear filtering operation
and can be mathematically expressed as a convolution. The impulse
response of the averaging filter is denoted as :math:`h_{avg}(t)`.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_ideal

     {s_{out}(t) = (h}_{avg}*s_{mix})(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{+ \infty}{h_{avg}(\tau)s_{mix}(t - \tau)}d\tau \\
     = A_{in}\ \left(  {\ e}^{i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} - f}_{ref})t + \varphi_{in} \right)}
     \int_{- \infty}^{+ \infty}{h_{avg}(\tau)\ e^{- i2{\pi(f_{in} - f}_{ref})\tau}d\tau} \right. \\
     \left. + {\ e}^{- i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} + f}_{ref})t+ \varphi_{in} \right)}\int_{- \infty}^{+ \infty}{h_{avg}
     (\tau)\ e^{i2{\pi(f_{in} + f}_{ref})\tau}d\tau} \right)


You may recognize the integral terms as Fourier transforms of the
filter’s impulse response, i.e., its frequency response function,
:math:`H_{avg}`.

.. math::
     :label: Fh_avg

     H_{avg}(f) = \int_{- \infty}^{+ \infty}{h_{avg}(\tau)\ e^{- i2\pi f\tau}d\tau} 
     = {\mathcal{F\{}\ h}_{avg}(\tau)\}(f)

Equation :eq:`s_out_ideal` can then be written more succinctly. The change is in
notation only.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_ideal_fourier

     s_{out}(t) = A_{in}\ \left( {\ e}^{i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} - f}_{ref})t + 
     \varphi_{in} \right)}H_{avg}{(f_{in} - f}_{ref}) + {\ e}^{- i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} + f}_{ref})t + 
     \varphi_{in} \right)}H_{avg}{( - f_{in} - f}_{ref}) \right)



Expression :eq:`s_out_ideal_fourier` shows that the amplitudes of 
the output’s two oscillating components are given by the filter’s 
response at their respective frequencies. Note that the filter serves 
a dual purpose. On the one hand, it selects the frequency band around the
reference frequency in which input frequencies are detected via
the first term of the sum. On the other hand, it eliminates the
second, fast undulating :math:`2f`-component, product of the
multiplication with the reference signal.

In practice, the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 uses a rectangular filter
function with adjustable size. In this introduction to lock-in amplification, however, we will
use an ideal low-pass filter instead. The intuition gained in this
simplified case remains largely valid in the detailed mathematical
description of a more realistic signal processing model, which can be
found in the following section.

The ideal “brick-wall” filter lets frequency components below a cut off,
:math:`f_{c}`, pass unaffected and removes all others completely. As is
discussed in most textbooks on signals and systems, the impulse
response, :math:`h_{avg}(t)`, of such a filter is the :math:`sinc`
function dilatated by the “averaging duration”, :math:`T`. The cutoff
frequency is inversely related to this dilation factor,
:math:`f_{c} = \frac{1}{2T}`.

.. math::
     :label: h_avg_ideal

     h_{avg}(t) = \frac{1}{T}sinc\left( \frac{t}{T} \right) \ \ \ \ \ \overset{\mathcal{F}}{\rightarrow} \ \ \ \ \ H_{avg}(f) = rect\left( \frac{f}{{2f}_{c}} \right)

.. math::
     \mbox{, where} \ \ \ \ \ sinc(x)\equiv \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} \ \ \ \ \ \mbox{and} \ \ \ \ \ rect(x) \equiv\left\{ \begin{matrix} \begin{matrix} 1 & \mbox{if} \ |x| < \frac{1}{2} \\ \end{matrix} \\ \begin{matrix} 0 & \mbox{otherwise} \\ \end{matrix} \\ \end{matrix} \right.


Plugging the frequency response of the ideal low-pass filter,
:math:`H_{avg}`, into equation :eq:`s_out_ideal_fourier` yields an expression for the
amplification output, :math:`s_{out}(t)`.

.. math::
     :label: h_avg_ideal_filter

     s_{out}(t) = A_{in}\ \left( {\ e}^{i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} - f}_{ref})t + \varphi_{in} \right)}rect\left( \frac{{f_{in} - f}_{ref}}{{2f}_{c}} \right)\  + {\ e}^{- i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} + f}_{ref})t + \varphi_{in} \right)}rect\left( \frac{{f_{in} + f}_{ref}}{2f_{c}} \right) \right)


The interplay between the frequency of the input, :math:`f_{in}`, the
reference frequency, :math:`f_{ref},` and the filter’s cut-off frequency
parameter, :math:`f_{c}`, determines the lock-in output.

The simplest case arises if the reference frequency, :math:`f_{ref}`,
matches the input frequency, :math:`f_{in}`, exactly and the filter
removes the fast-rotating :math:`2f`-component.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_ideal_0

     s_{out}(t) = A_{in}\ {\ e}^{i\varphi_{in}} \ \ \ \mbox{if} \ \ \ {f_{in} = f}_{ref} \ \ \ \mbox{and} \ \ \ {{f_{c} < f}_{in} + f}_{ref}


This is the principal output of a dual-phase lock-in amplifier (see
:numref:`LIA_monofreq` a). The result is a complex constant with amplitude and phase
given by the amplitude and the phase shift of the input sinusoid before
lock-in amplification, respectively.

In the general case :math:`{f_{in} \neq f}_{ref}`, three outcomes (see
:numref:`LIA_monofreq` b) can be distinguished based on the filter’s cut off
frequency, :math:`f_{c}`.

I. When averaging over a long interval compared to the period of the
   signal’s two frequency components, both slow- and fast-rotating
   signal terms are stripped away.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_ideal_I

     s_{out}(t) = 0 \ \ \ \mbox{if} \ \ \  f_{c} < \left| {f_{in} - f}_{ref} \right|

In this incident, the sinusoid input signal is outside the frequency
band around :math:`f_{ref}`, which gets detected. If :math:`f_{c}` tends
to zero, all but oscillations at the reference frequency are rejected.

II. As the “averaging duration” shortens, :math:`f_{c}` increases and at
    first, the slower oscillation alone is extracted from the mixed
    signal.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_ideal_II

     s_{out}(t) = A_{in}\ {\ e}^{i\left( 2{\pi(f_{in} - f}_{ref})t + \varphi_{in} \right)} \ \ \ \mbox{if} \ \ \  \left|{f_{in} - f}_{ref} \right|{{< f_{c} < f}_{in} + f}_{ref}


Note that the amplitude of the output oscillation is the same as that of
the input sinusoid, :math:`s_{in}`, which can therefore be retrieved.
Its frequency, however, is given by the difference between the input
frequency and the reference frequency, which is in general much lower
than that of the input. This outcome is sometimes called a “beat” in
analogy to the well-known phenomenon in acoustics occurring when two
waves with slightly different frequencies are superimposed. Expression
:eq:`s_out_ideal_0` is a particular instance of the more general case II.

III. If averaging occurs over too short a period, the fast-rotating term
     introduced by the mixing is not removed either. The output is the
     same as the signal before filtering.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_ideal_III

     s_{out}(t) = s_{mix}(t) \ \ \ \mbox{if} \ \ \  f_{in} + f_{ref} < f_{c}

To sum up, the case distinction implies for practical lock-in
applications in general that:

-  The reference frequency, :math:`f_{ref}`, should be chosen to match
   the input frequency, :math:`f_{in}`.

-  The filter time constant :math:`T`, or equivalently the bandwidth,
   should be picked so that case II is attained.

Adjusting the filter size and hence :math:`f_{c}`, may change the result
of lock-in amplification more gradually if the input does not consist of
a single sinusoid. Yet the result with a sine wave input can easily be
extended to more complex input signals; as we will see next, they can be decomposed into
their frequency components by Fourier analysis (see :numref:`LIA_monofreq` c).

.. figure:: img/lock_in_principle_FD.png
      :name: LIA_monofreq
      :align: center
      :width: 97.5%

      Dual-Phase Lock-In Amplification with Monofrequent Inputs

      The idealized signal processing pipeline of lock-in detection is shown
      sequentially from left to right. The real input signal, :math:`s_{in}`,
      is multiplicatively mixed with a complex sinusoid reference signal,
      :math:`s_{ref}`, which oscillates at the reference frequency
      :math:`f_{ref}`. Subsequently, the mixed signal, :math:`s_{mix}`, is
      averaged with an ideal low-pass filter with impulse response
      :math:`h_{ref}`. :math:`s_{out}` is complex; its real and the imaginary
      part, are respectively called the “in-phase”, :math:`I,`
      and “quadrature”, :math:`Q,` component.

      a. Time domain (TD) view of lock-in detection with a sinusoid input in
         the special case where its frequency :math:`f_{in}` is equal to
         :math:`f_{ref}` and the filter size is adequate. The output is a
         complex constant whose amplitude and phase are given by the amplitude
         and phase shift of :math:`s_{in}`. Note that
         :math:`I(t) = Re\left\lbrack s_{out}(t) \right\rbrack` and
         :math:`Q(t) = Im\left\lbrack s_{out}(t) \right\rbrack`.

      b. The general case of a sinusoid input when
         :math:`f_{in} \neq f_{ref}` with cases distinguished by the
         adjustable filter size in TD.

         I :math:`f_{in}` is outside the frequency band around :math:`f_{res}` that passes the filter. This input is hence rejected and :math:`s_{out}` is zero.

         II With a shorter “averaging period”, the input gets detected. The modulation frequency of sinusoid :math:`s_{out}` is :math:`f_{in} - f_{ref}`.

         III If the averaging duration is too short, the undesirable high-frequency component in :math:`s_{mix}` is still present in :math:`s_{out}`.

      c. Fourier domain (FD) view of lock-in detection with a
         multifrequential, amplitude modulated input. The reference
         frequency, :math:`f_{ref}`, is equal to that of the sinusoid carrier
         signal, :math:`f_{car}`. Frequency components treated individually in a. and b. are highlighted in blue and red, respectively. The same
         filters I-III as in b. are considered. Only with filter II is the
         modulation signal retrieved faithfully.

.. admonition:: Conversion to Polar Coordinates

   By default, the lock-in output is in cartesian coordinates. Its real and 
   imaginary parts are named “in-phase” :math:`I\left(t\right)` and the 
   “quadrature” :math:`Q\left(t\right)` components after their respective 
   phase relationship with the local oscillator, that is driving the 
   reference signal.

   .. math::
     :label: I_and_Q

     I\left(t\right)=Re\left[s_{out}\left(t\right)\right]

     Q\left(t\right)=Im\left[s_{out}\left(t\right)\right]

   A conversion to polar coordinates yields amplitude and phase terms, 
   which are often more natural to interpret.

   .. math::
     :label: A_and_Phi

     A\left(t\right)=\sqrt{I^2\left(t\right)+Q^2\left(t\right)}

     \varphi\left(t\right)=arctan2\left(Q\left(t\right),I\left(t\right)\right)

Frequency Domain
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Lock-in amplification is linear, meaning the system’s response to a
signal is equal to the sum of the responses to its constituent parts.
Signals can generally be expressed as a continuous sum of complex
sinusoids with different frequencies and phases. The Fourier transform
determines how strongly each of these components are present in a signal
– the frequency domain representation of a signal.

.. math::
     :label: Fourier_Transform

     \int_{- \infty}^{+ \infty}{x(t)\ e^{- i 2\pi ft}dt} = \mathcal{F}\left\{ x(t) \right\}(f) = X(f)

By Fourier transforming input, intermediary and processing signals, the
chain of operations in lock-in detection introduced above can be
analyzed in the frequency domain. Because its action is
frequency-specific by design, lock-in amplification is inherently
well-describable in the frequency domain.

Noisy, Amplitude Modulated Input
''''''''''''''''''''''''''''''''

An amplitude modulated signal, :math:`s_{am}`, consists of a
sinusoid carrier wave, :math:`s_{car}`, whose amplitude is varied or
*modulated* slowly compared to its oscillation frequency (see :numref:`AM_DP_LIA`
a). These amplitude variations bear the information contained in the
signal. The time evolution of the amplitude is called the modulation
signal, :math:`s_{mod}`.

.. math::
 :label: s_am

     s_{am}(t) = A(t)\cdot cos\left( 2{\pi f}_{car}t + \varphi_{car} \right) 
     = s_{mod}(t) \cdot s_{car}(t) 

Demodulation of such signals, with variable amplitude and thus more than
a single frequency component, is a common application of lock-in
amplification. It can be achieved by matching the reference frequency to
the carrier frequency. Realistic inputs moreover contain background
noise. So, let us examine an arbitrary amplitude modulated signal,
:math:`s_{am}`, with additive noise, :math:`s_{\sigma}`, as input,
:math:`s_{in}`.

.. math::
 :label: s_noise

     s_{in}(t) = s_{am}(t) + s_{\sigma}(t)


We would like to perform our analysis in the Frequency domain and must
therefore find the Fourier transform of :math:`s_{in}`. It is given by
the sum of the Fourier transforms of :math:`s_{in}`\ ’s addends since
the Fourier transform is a linear operation :eq:`lc`.

.. math::
 :label: S_noise_FD
     
     S_{in}(f) = S_{am}(f) + S_{\sigma}(f)


:math:`S_{am}` can be stated in terms of :math:`S_{mod}`, the carrier
frequency, :math:`f_{car}`, and phase shift, :math:`\varphi_{car}`.
Expressions :eq:`lc`, :eq:`mu`, :eq:`c_sinusoid`, and :eq:`dirac_shift` from summary on the
Fourier transform are used in conjunction with the complex form of the
cosine :eq:`cos_euler` to derive :math:`S_{am}` in this form from its time domain
expression :eq:`s_am`.

.. math::
 :label: S_am_FD
     
     S_{am}(f)&=S_{mod}(f)*S_{car}(f) \\
     &= S_{mod}(f)*\mathcal{F}\left\{ \cos\left( 2{\pi f}_{car}t + \varphi_{car} \right) \right\}(f) \\
     &= S_{mod}(f)*\mathcal{F}\left\{ \frac{e^{i\left(2{\pi f}_{car}t + \varphi_{car} \right)} + e^{- i\left(2{\pi f}_{car}t + \varphi_{car} \right)}}{2} \right\}(f) \\
     &= S_{mod}(f)*\frac{e^{i\varphi_{car}}\mathcal{F}\left\{e^{i2{\pi f}_{car}t} \right\}(f) + e^{- i\varphi_{car}}\mathcal{F}\left\{ e^{- i2{\pi f}_{car}t} \right\}(f)}{2} \\
     &= S_{mod}(f)*\frac{e^{i\varphi_{car}}\delta\left( f - f_{car} \right) +e^{- i\varphi_{car}}\delta\left( f + f_{car} \right)}{2} \\
     &= \frac{{e^{i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f - f_{car} \right) + {e^{- i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f + f_{car} \right)}{2}



As the last term reveals, the frequency domain description of amplitude
modulation is that it produces a signal with power concentrated in two
bands around plus and minus the carrier frequency.

Plugging :eq:`S_am_FD` into :eq:`S_noise_FD` yields the frequency domain expression of the
noisy, amplitude-modulated input to the lock-in amplifier.

.. math::
 :label: S_in_FD

     S_{in}(f) = \frac{e^{i\varphi_{car}} S_{mod}\left( f - f_{car} \right) + {e^{- i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f + f_{car} \right)}{2} + S_{\sigma}(f)



.. figure:: img/lock_in_amplification_FD_noise.png
      :name: AM_DP_LIA
      :align: center
      :width: 100%

      Amplitude Modulation and Ideal Dual-Phase Demodulation with Noise

      In both a. and b., time-domain (TD) signals :math:`s_{\cdot}(t)` are
      depicted alongside their frequency domain (FD) representations
      :math:`S_{\cdot}(f).`

      a.  Synthesis of an Amplitude Modulated Signal with Noise in TD (left)
          and FD (right).

          ..

          The amplitude of a sinusoid carrier signal :math:`s_{car}` is varied
          in time according to a modulation signal :math:`s_{mod}`. The result
          is the amplitude modulated signal, :math:`s_{am}.` The FD equivalent
          is a convolution of the modulation spectrum, :math:`S_{mod}`, with
          that of the carrier wave, :math:`S_{car}`. The ensuing signal in FD is a
          duplicated and generally phase-shifted version of :math:`S_{mod}`
          with half the original amplitude, whose two component parts are
          shifted to +/- the carrier frequency, :math:`f_{car}`. Broadband
          noise, :math:`s_{\sigma}`, is added to the signal :math:`s_{am}` yielding
          the input signal :math:`s_{in}`.

      b.  Dual-Phase Demodulation of Noisy, Amplitude Modulated Signal in TD
          (left) and FD (right).

          ..

          The noisy, amplitude modulated input signal, :math:`s_{in}`, from a.
          is demodulated with an ideal dual-phase lock-in amplifier. First, the
          input, :math:`s_{in}`, is multiplied with a complex sinusoid
          reference signal whose frequency is equal to :math:`f_{car}`. With
          the effect in the frequency domain in mind, this step is called
          down-mixing. The spectrum of the product of mixing, :math:`S_{mix}`,
          is shifted by :math:`f_{ref}` with respect to that of the
          input\ :math:`\ S_{in}`. In this fashion, one of :math:`S_{in}`\ ’s
          two spectral components corresponding to :math:`S_{mod}` comes to lie
          in the baseband. :math:`S_{mix}` is then low-pass filtered so that
          only the baseband passes. The ideal “brick-wall” filter,
          :math:`H_{avg}`, lets frequency components below the cut off pass
          freely and completely blocks all others. As the noise is much more
          broadband than the signal, much of it gets removed along with the
          high-frequency copy of :math:`S_{in}`. The modulation signal with
          little remaining noise is given by the amplitude of the complex
          output, :math:`s_{out}`.

Down-Mixing
'''''''''''

Fourier relation :eq:`c_sinusoid` spells out that the Fourier transform of
:math:`s_{ref}(t)`, given by :eq:`s_ref_ideal`, is a frequency-shifted Delta Dirac
distribution scaled by a factor two.

.. math:: 
     :label: S_ref_FD
          
          S_{ref}(f) = 2\delta\left( f + f_{ref} \right)


The first step in demodulation (see :numref:`AM_DP_LIA` b) - multiplication of the
input with the reference signal - is a convolution of the two in the
frequency domain :eq:`mu`. It can be computed using :eq:`dirac_shift`. This step
introduces negative shift of the frequency spectrum by :math:`f_{ref}`
hence the name down-mixing.

.. math:: 
     :label: S_mix_FD
          
          S_{mix}(f) = S_{in}(f)*S_{ref}(f) = S_{in}(f)*2\delta\left(f + f_{ref} \right) = 2S_{in}\left( f + f_{ref} \right)


With the amplitude modulated input under consideration, :math:`S_{mix}` is given by the
following expression.

.. math:: 
     :label: S_mix_FD_002
          
          S_{mix}(f) = {e^{i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f - f_{car}{+ f}_{ref} \right) + {e^{- i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f + f_{car} + f_{ref} \right)+ \ 2\ S_{\sigma}\left( f + f_{ref} \right)



If the reference frequency approximately matches the carrier frequency,
one part of the duplicated frequency spectrum comes to lie in the
baseband. The other portion is shifted to a highly negative frequency
band. If in addition\ :math:`\ f_{ref} = f_{car}`, the baseband
component of the signal coincides with the spectrum of the modulation
signal up to a phase shift. (The noise is shifted in frequency too.)

Low-Pass Filtering
''''''''''''''''''

At this point, the purpose of the filtering step in lock-in
amplification is straightforward. The baseband component of the signal,
the first term in equation :eq:`S_mix_FD_002`, is the only one of interest. To
retrieve the modulation signal, its high-frequency copy and as much
noise as possible ought to be removed by a low-pass filter.

In the frequency domain, applying a low-pass filter amounts to
multiplying with its frequency response, :math:`H_{avg}`. Lock-in
amplification is thus captured by a simple equation, which expresses a
frequency shift of the input followed multiplicative,
frequency-dependent scaling of spectral components.

.. math:: 
     :label: S_out_FD
          
          S_{out}(f) = S_{mix}(f){\times H}_{avg}(f)= 2S_{in}\left( f + f_{ref} \right){\times H}_{avg}(f)




Inserting expressions for the frequency response of the ideal low-pass
filter :eq:`h_avg_ideal` and :math:`S_{mix}` :eq:`S_mix_FD_002`, yields the output when used in
demodulation of a noisy signal.

.. math:: 
     :label: S_out_FD_002
          
          S_{out}(f) = {e^{i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f - f_{car}{+ f}_{ref}\right) \cdot rect\left( \frac{f}{{2f}_{c}} \right) \\
          + {{\ e}^{- i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f + f_{car} + f_{ref}\right) \cdot rect\left( \frac{f}{{2f}_{c}} \right) \\
          + \ 2{\cdot S}_{\sigma}\left( f + f_{ref}\right) \cdot rect\left( \frac{f}{{2f}_{c}} \right)

          

Ignoring the noise term for now and assuming a finite modulation
bandwidth, three cases depending on filter cut-off may be distinguished,
analogous to those discussed in time-domain (see :numref:`LIA_monofreq` c):

I.  If the filter bandwidth is too narrow compared to the frequency
    spectrum of modulation signal, a non-negligible part of its power
    can be chucked away by the filter. A distorted version of the
    modulation signal is retrieved.

II. With an appropriate cut-off frequency of the ideal filter, the
    baseband term alone is extracted. This is the usual, desired
    behavior in dual-phase demodulation.

    .. math:: 
     :label: S_out_FD_003
          
          S_{out}(f) = {e^{i\varphi_{car}}S}_{mod}\left( f - f_{car}{+ f}_{ref} \right) 


    Using the modulation property :eq:`mo`, the time-domain output can be
    deduced from :eq:`S_out_FD_003`.

    .. math:: 
     :label: S_out_FD_004
               
          s_{out}(t) = {e^{i{(2\pi(f_{car}{- f}_{ref})t + \varphi}_{car})}s}_{mod}(t)  




    The oscillatory component in :eq:`S_out_FD_004` is generally of low frequency or
    entirely constant, as :math:`{\ f}_{ref}` is usually chosen to match
    :math:`f_{car}`. Converting the output to polar
    coordinates allows to retrieve the original modulation signal in the
    lock-in amplitude.

    .. math:: 
     :label: S_out_FD_005
               
          A_{out}(t) &= s_{mod}(t) \\
          \varphi_{out}(t) &= {2\pi\left( f_{car}{- f}_{ref} \right)t + \varphi}_{car}   


III. Using a filter that is too broad, high frequency components from
     the :math:`2f` spectral component may enter the output.

The frequency domain picture also illustrates nicely how noise is
suppressed by the filter (see :numref:`AM_DP_LIA` b). Noise is usually more
broadband than the modulation signal. Wherever there is no overlap with
extracted modulation spectrum, it can be removed without penalty. The
noise spectrum and its overlap with the signal give therefore a
supplementary criterion for tuning the filter bandwidth.

Smart Pixel Image Sensor
------------------------

The lock-in principle presented above is implemented directly in the
heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4’s specially designed camera sensor, the
proprietary heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4. Its pixel sensor units register the
light intensity in a precisely clocked, periodic fashion and promptly
process acquired signals. In the following, several sensor-related
concepts are introduced, which may help with the operation of the
lock-in camera.

.. _Temporal_Sequence:

Temporal Sequence
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

We have seen that lock-in detection uses prior knowledge about the time evolution of a
signal's carrier to extract only the slow-varying, information-bearing
part. Thus, the exact control of the temporal sequence of processing
steps is predictably crucial for the realization of this principle in a
physical system.

Trigger Signals
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

The heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 relies on trigger signals to schedule its
operation (:numref:`sensor_principle` a). These control signals are tightly linked to
configuration options. Understanding of how they structure the lock-in
process helps exploit the full performance of the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4.
Trigger signals can be generated camera-internally and then operate
unbeknownst to the user. However, many experiments require pinpoint
temporal synchronization to other elements of the set up. For these
cases, the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 offers the possibility to provide
external trigger signals.

Period
^^^^^^

The heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 initiates a measurement upon receiving a
recording trigger. Thereafter, the pace is dictated by the reference
trigger signal, a pulse wave which by default determines - roughly
speaking - the start of a new period of the reference signal. In the
heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4, this period also imposes constraints on the
filtering and sampling during analogue to digital conversion.

Frame
^^^^^

The optical input generally depends on the location of the pixel on the
image sensor that records it. Each pixel can be interpreted to perform a
multiplication of the local input, :math:`s_{in}(t,x,y)`, with a spatially uniform, effective
reference signal, :math:`s_{ref}(t)`, followed by an integration over a
selectable number of full periods, :math:`N_{P}`. The result of this
averaging is a frame (with complex pixel values due to the dual-phase
nature of the lock-in implementation). Multiple frames are acquired in
instant succession.

In short, a single pixel with discrete indices :math:`x` and :math:`y`, according
to its spatial location in pixel coordinates, takes the following value
in the :math:`m^{th}` frame.

.. math:: 
     :label: s_out_frame_001
          
         s_{out,\ m}\lbrack x,y\rbrack = \int_{t_{m} - \frac{T_{m}}{2}}^{t_{m} + \frac{T_{m}}{2}}{s_{in}(t,x,y)s_{ref}(t)dt} 


Here, :math:`t_{m}` is the timestamp at the center of the interval
feeding into the frame and :math:`T_{m}` is its duration. :math:`T_{m}`
is given by the sum of the periods making up frame :math:`m`.

Lock-in amplification in the heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 can be brought in
line with the view presented in the previous section, by expressing the
output as a sequence of a mixing, a filtering, and a sampling step. To
this end, the integral over a finite interval in :eq:`s_out_frame_001` is restated as a
convolution with a rectangular function evaluated a single point in
time.

.. math:: 
     :label: s_out_frame_002
          
         s_{out,\ m}\lbrack x,y\rbrack = \left( h_{avg}*\left( s_{in}{(x,y) \times s}_{ref} \right) \right)\left( t_{m} \right)


, where :math:`h_{avg}` is the impulse response of a central moving
average filter with window length :math:`T_{m}`.

.. math:: 
     :label: h_avg_frame
          
         h_{avg}(t) = rect\left( \frac{t}{T_{m}} \right)


With an irregular reference trigger, the starts of a period are uneven. 
But normally, periods are of uniform duration so that
the filter width is also a constant, :math:`T = T_{m}\ \forall\ m\ `. In
this case, which is presumed from here onwards, frame acquisition is
regularly spaced in time with sampling period :math:`T`.

Quarter Period
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Again, the heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 performs *dual-phase* demodulation. As
in the previous introduction to lock-in amplification, the notation of
this twofold process is condensed using a single, yet complex reference
signal, :math:`s_{ref}` (see :numref:`sensor_principle` b). Its imaginary part,
accounting for the second demodulation, is also shifted by a quarter
period with respect to the real part. Both these signal components are
well-approximated by a pulse wave with two short, equally spaced pulses
per period - every other pulse having a negative sign. :math:`s_{ref}`
‘s fundamental frequency is the reference frequency :math:`f_{ref}` (cf.
:numref:`lock_in_amplification_real`).

Since by default, the external reference trigger signal is deemed to
oscillate between two values *at the reference frequency*, it ordinarily
signals the onset of a period. Yet to realize the effective reference
signal, :math:`s_{ref}`, the heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 needs an indication
of the start of each quarter period. This timing information is
extracted from the external reference trigger by a programmable
processing unit for frequency scaling to a power of two. With baseline
settings it performs frequency multiplication by four behind the scenes.

.. _in_pixel_processing:

Processing in Pixel
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Understanding how the camera sensor, heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4, uses this
derived quarter-period trigger to do lock-in amplification with a pulse
wave as reference, demands an additional information layer.

Like other image sensors, the heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 is an array of
light-sensitive semiconductor devices, called photodiodes, which convert
the incidence of photons to an electric current. Photodiodes integrate
the intensity of the light they receive in a time interval. In the
heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4, they do so over the duration of single quarter
periods (see :numref:`sensor_principle` c). The fraction of the full quarter period
during which the photodiode is sensitive to light, :math:`p_{s}`, is
configurable up to a hardware overhead of :math:`< 2\mu s`.

The signal thus retrieved in each quarter period constitutes the basic
building block from which the lock-in output is synthesized by simple
addition and subtraction.

.. math:: 
     :label: s_qp
          
         s_{m,n,qp}\lbrack x,y\rbrack = \int_{p_{s} \times t_{{qp}_{m,n}}}^{}{s_{in}(t,x,y)dt}


, where :math:`t_{qp}` is the duration of a quarter period, identified
unambiguously by indices :math:`m`, :math:`n` and :math:`qp`.

-  :math:`m \in \left\{ 1,...,N_{F} \right\}` specifies in which of
   :math:`N_{F}` frames acquired the quarter period is situated.

-  :math:`n \in \left\{ 1,...,N_{P} \right\}` indicates in which of the
   :math:`N_{P}` periods of frame :math:`m` the quarter period is
   situated.

-  :math:`qp \in \left\{ I,II,II,IV \right\}` specifies which quarter
   period in period :math:`n` it is\ *.*

From this element, a complex lock-in signal per period is computed.

.. math:: 
     :label: s_p

     s_{m,n}\lbrack x,y\rbrack = s_{m,n,I}\lbrack x,y\rbrack + {i\cdot s}_{m,n,II}\lbrack x,y\rbrack - s_{m,n,III}\lbrack x,y\rbrack - {i \cdot s}_{m,n,IV}\lbrack x,y\rbrack

In each pixel, the contributions of :math:`N_{P}` individual periods are
summed up into a frame.

.. math:: 
     :label: s_out_frame_003

     
     s_{out,\ \ m}\lbrack x,y\rbrack = \sum_{n = 1}^{N_{P}}{s_{m,n}\lbrack x,y\rbrack} 

These sums and differences of the basic element
:math:`s_{m,n,qp}\lbrack x,y\rbrack` can be executed with low power
dissipation and small footprint within the architecture of the
individual smart pixel.

      :name: sensor_principle
      :align: center
      :width: 97.5%

      Operational Sequence in the heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4

      a.  The recording trigger prompts the start of a recording. Subsequently, 
          the beginning of each period of the effective reference signal is 
          prompted by the reference trigger. A frame holds a complex value 
          for each pixel. It is the result of integrating the input mixed 
          with the complex, effective reference signal, :math:`s_{ref}`, over an 
          adaptable number of full periods. :math:`T` is both the window width of 
          the equivalent central moving average filter, :math:`h_{avg}`, and the 
          sampling period.
	 
      b.  The heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 needs a trigger impulse every quarter period 
          to realize the effective signal as it is a modified complex pulse 
          wave with rectangular impulses at that same rate. By default, the 
          input reference trigger is meant to oscillate with the reference 
          frequency, which is normally chosen to match the input’s carrier 
          frequency. The desired quarter period trigger is deduced from the 
          reference trigger via frequency scaling.
	 
      c.  In every quarter period, the image sensor’s photodiodes integrate 
          the intensity of the incident light. Except for an overhead, the 
          light-sensitive proportion of the full period is configurable. The 
          acquired signal in a single pixel forms the basic unit from 
          which the lock-in output at that site is assembled via simple summation 
          and subtraction. Although intermediate steps are executed in a different 
          order, the output is strictly the same as when described in terms of 
          multiplicative mixing with :math:`s_{ref}` and convolutive filtering with
          :math:`h_{avg}`.Please note that the schematic illustrates the scenario

Effective Signal Processing
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The following paragraph offers an implementation-oriented model of the
signal processing in a single pixel of the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4, which
is more accurate than the previous analysis of ideal lock-in
amplification. For advanced experiments, the implications of this
specific implementation may become significant for an informed choice of
camera settings and accurate interpretation of measurement results. The
preceding explanation of the image sensor’s operation principle implies
two consequential deviations from the ideal; both the reference signal,
:math:`s_{ref}`, and the filter’s impulse response, :math:`h_{avg}`,
differ from their ideal counterparts.

Mixing with a Pulse Wave
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

As argued above, a pulse wave is a good approximation of the effective
reference signal of the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4. This pulsed approximation
of :math:`s_{ref}` can be represented analytically in a plethora of
ways. The option chosen here is conceptually close to the hardware
implementation. :math:`s_{ref}` is viewed as the convolution of the
rectangular sensor response in a single quarter period and a modified
Dirac comb (see :numref:`lock_in_amplification_real` a).

.. math:: 
     :label: s_ref_real

     s_{ref}(t) = 2 \cdot rect\left( \frac{t}{\frac{p_{s}}{4f_{ref}}} \right)*\left( e^{- i2\pi f_{ref}t} \cdot \sum_{n\mathbb{\in Z}}{\delta\left( t - \frac{n}{4f_{ref}} \right)} \right) 



The sensor is uniformly responsive in a fraction :math:`p_{s}` of the
full quarter period :math:`\left( 4 \cdot f_{ref} \right)^{- 1}`,
which determines the width of the first, “boxcar”-like function. The
spacing between spikes of the modified Dirac comb is a quarter period,
indicating the timing of individual acquisitions. Spikes are alternately
real and imaginary. Their sign flips after each consecutive pair to
account for subtraction and summation quarter-periodic measurement
results.

An advantage of this complicated representation is that its frequency
domain representation can be broken down to basic relations :eq:`co`,
:eq:`mo`, :eq:`rect` and :eq:`dirac_comb`, found in the summary on the Fourier Transform.

.. math:: 
     :label: s_ref_real_FD

     S_{ref}(f) &= \mathcal{F}\left\{ 2 \cdot rect\left( \frac{t}{\frac{p_{s}}{4f_{ref}}} \right) \right\}(f) 
     \times \mathcal{F}\left\{ e^{- i2\pi f_{ref}t} \times 
     \sum_{n\mathbb{\in Z}}^{}{\delta\left( t - \frac{n}{4f_{ref}} \right)} \right\}(f) \\ 
     &= 2\sum_{k\mathbb{\in Z}}^{}{p_{s}sinc\left( p_{s}\left( k - \frac{1}{4} \right) \right) \times \delta\left( f + f_{ref} - 4kf_{ref} \right)}   




In contrast to the ideal case, the reference signal comprises also
non-zero components at frequencies other than
:math:`( - {)1 \times f}_{ref}`, namely at
:math:`( + ){3 \times f}_{ref},\ ( - {)5 \times f}_{ref},( - {)7 \times f}_{ref}`
and so forth. They are called odd harmonics of the fundamental frequency
:math:`f_{ref}`. The :math:`sinc` term determines the magnitude with
which these frequencies appear in the reference signal. Although higher
frequencies components tend to be attenuated more, not all higher odd
harmonics are insignificant compared to the first harmonic. The
potential to selectively reduce their prominence is limited by the fact
that :math:`p_{s}`, being the fraction of the quarter period in which
photodiodes are sensitive, cannot exceed 1.

Mixing is described analogous to :eq:`s_mix_ideal` in time- and to :eq:`S_mix_FD` in frequency
domain (see :numref:`lock_in_amplification_real` b).



.. math::
     :label: s_mix_real

     s_{mix}(t) = s_{in}(t) \times s_{ref}(t) = s_{in}(t) \times 2\sum_{n\mathbb{\in Z}}^{}{e^{- i2\pi\frac{n}{4}}rect\left( \frac{4f_{ref}t - n}{p_{s}} \right)}
.. math::  

     \Bigg\downarrow\mathcal{F}

.. math::

     S_{mix}(f) = S_{in}(f)*S_{ref}(f) = 2\sum_{k\mathbb{\in Z}}^{}{p_{s}sinc\left( p_{s}\left(k - \frac{1}{4} \right) \right) \times S_{in}\left( f + f_{ref} - 4kf_{ref} \right)}


.. figure:: img/lock_in_amplification_real.png
     :name: lock_in_amplification_real
     :align: center
     :width: 100%

     Effective Reference Signal and Dual-Phase Demodulation

     In both a. and b., time-domain (TD) signals :math:`s_{\cdot}(t)` are
     depicted left alongside their frequency domain (FD) representations
     :math:`S_{\cdot}(f)` on the right.

     a.   Synthesis of the Effective Reference Signal in TD (left) and FD
          (right) from elementary components.
          
          In TD, a Dirac comb indicating
          quarter periodic sampling is multiplied with the ideal complex
          reference oscillation to indicate how individual samples are weighted
          when summed together. The result is convolved with the rectangular
          sensor sensitivity response in a single quarter period to yield the
          effective reference signal, :math:`s_{ref}` of the image sensor
          heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4. :math:`s_{ref}` differs from the ideal case in that it
          contains non-zero frequency components at odd harmonics of the
          fundamental reference frequency, :math:`f_{ref}`. Moreover, the
          salience of all these components, inclusively that at reference
          frequency, is given by the :math:`sinc` of their frequency.

     b.   Demodulation of an amplitude modulated input with a complex pulse
          wave as reference and a rectangular filter.
          
          The demodulation process
          is analogous to the ideal case but for a different reference signal
          and filter. The filter width is also the sampling period. This final
          sampling step is not shown. In contrast to the ideal case, mixing not only shifts the input spectrum
          by the reference frequency. Each odd harmonic component in the reference
          signal produces a frequency-shifted copy, according to its own
          magnitude. Input frequency components around each odd multiple of the
          reference frequency end up in the baseband. The lock-in amplifiers that
          use a pulse wave such as the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 are therefore
          sensitive to odd harmonics of the reference frequency.



Rectangular Filter and Sampling
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

The frequency response of the rectangular averaging filter :eq:`h_avg_frame` is a
:math:`sinc` according to :eq:`rect`.

.. math::
     :label: H_avg_FD_real

     H_{avg}(f) = T \cdot sinc(T \cdot f) 


The (continuous) output signal is obtained by convolution of the mixed
signal :math:`s_{mix}` with the filter’s impulse response
:math:`h_{avg}` (see :numref:`lock_in_amplification_real` b) like in the ideal case :eq:`s_out_ideal`. In the
frequency domain, again, this corresponds to a multiplication with the
filter’s frequency response :math:`H_{avg}`.

.. math::
     :label: s_out_real

     s_{out}(t) &= h_{avg}(t)*s_{mix}(t) \\ &= 
     2 \cdot rect\left( \frac{t}{T} \right)*\left( s_{in}(t) \times 
     \sum_{n\mathbb{\in Z}}^{}{e^{- i2\pi\frac{n}{4}}rect\left( \frac{4f_{ref}t- n}{p_{s}} \right)} \right)

.. math::  

     \Bigg\downarrow\mathcal{F}

.. math::

     S_{out}(f) &= H_{avg}(f) \times S_{mix}(f)\\  
     &= 2 \cdot T \cdot sinc(T \cdot f) \times \sum_{k\mathbb{\in Z}}{p_{s}
     sinc\left( p_{s}\left( k - \frac{1}{4} \right) \right) \cdot S_{in}\left( f + f_{ref}- 4kf_{ref} \right)}


The low-pass filter still extracts the baseband after the input is
subjected to down-mixing. Yet unlike an ideal filter, not all the
baseband components are treated with precise uniformity. They rather
pass with the :math:`sinc` of the frequency. Moreover, the filter cannot
selectively remove generally undesirable parts of the signal -
associated with higher odd harmonics - from the baseband.

The output of the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 is not actually continuous but
expression :eq:`s_out_real` is sampled with time constant :math:`T`, the filter
width. In the frequency domain, sampling is a periodization of the
spectrum and aliasing occurs if the modulation signal’s highest
frequency is above the Nyquist frequency.

.. _background_suppression:

Background Suppression
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Lock-in amplification, as presented above, is in principle insensitive
to large DC offsets on input signals (see :numref:`bg_suppression` a). By measuring
differences in input intensity at different moments during the period, a
constant offset theoretically cancels out.

In practice, the dynamic range of image sensors is limited (see :numref:`bg_suppression` b). 
If photodiodes register too many photon arrivals in one go, they
saturate. As uniformly saturated measurements propagate through the
in-pixel processing, they can erroneously result in an absence of AC
signal.

To circumvent this issue, the heliCam\ :sup:`TM` C4 offers a background
suppression mechanism (see :numref:`bg_suppression` c). If background suppression is
enabled, the first period in each frame is reserved to register the
input intensity (at the end of the period). This value is then
subtracted as background from the input during the rest of the frame,
when lock-in amplification is performed - thus effectively preventing
saturation due to large constant background signals. Suppressing the
background is only recommended if the DC offset is much larger than the
AC amplitude.

This background suppression is referred to as AC coupling because its
implementation is analogous to the namesake recording modality of a
conventional lock-in amplifier or an oscilloscope. Consequentially, the
operation mode without background suppression is called DC coupling.

.. _reference_signal_synthesis:

Reference Signal Synthesis
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Once the functionality of the sensor is understood, the parametrization
of the effective reference signal through lock-in camera features can be
explained clearly (cf. :numref:`graphical_summary`). Such parameters and important
selection options are printed in **bold** below.

As described earlier, the basic building block of the sensor schedule is
the quarter period. The pace of the reference signal, to which the input is compared, is
determined by a trigger indicating the start of each new quarter period.
How this trigger is obtained, depends on the **Reference Source Type:**

-  **Internal:** The heliCam\ :sup:`TM` ’s internal clock signals the
   onset of each quarter period. Another quarter period is automatically
   initiated when the sensor operation in the preceding one is finished.
   The fixed time it takes the sensor to run a quarter
   periodic measurement sequence, :math:`t'_{qp}`, is derived from the
   configurable **Reference Frequency,**
   :math:`\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}`.

   .. math::
     :label: t_qp

     t'_{qp} = \frac{1}{ 4 \cdot \mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}} }\ \ \ \ \ (\mathbf{internal}\ reference)
   
   In this case, the **Reference Frequency** feature value, :math:`\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}`, corresponds
   directly to the frequency of the effective reference signal,
   :math:`f_{ref}`. Equivalently, :math:`t'_{qp}` coincides with the effective quarter period duration, :math:`t_{qp}`.

   The camera feature **Sensitivity** determines the fraction of
   :math:`t'_{qp}` during which the sensor is light-sensitive and
   corresponds to :math:`p_{s}` from the previous paragraph.

   .. math::
     :label: t_s

     t_{s} = p_{s} \times t'_{qp} 


   , were :math:`t_{s}` denotes the photoreceptive portion of the quarter
   period. Viewed like this, the **Sensitivity** controls the duty cycle of
   the effective reference pulse wave.

-  **External:** A reference input (rect wave, 0-5 V) from an external source, potentially
   processed camera-internally, triggers the onset of a new quarter
   period in the responsive period after the completion of the fixed
   sensor operation sequence in its predecessor. This trigger – and not
   the set **Reference Frequency** – dictates :math:`s_{ref}`\ ’s
   effective frequency in the external mode. Additional parameters
   influence the effective reference signal.

   -     The **Reference Frequency Scaler** multiplies or divides the
         frequency of the trigger input by a select factor,
         :math:`\mathbf{M}` - in addition to a multiplication by
         four since the sensor needs indication of the quarter period
         rather than the period onset. With all factors
         :math:`\mathbf{M}` other than “DivideBy4”, establishing the
         reference frequency thus involves averaging incoming discrete
         pulses (evaluated once immediately prior to the onset of the recording). 
         Alone with the setting :math:`\mathbf{M}` = “DivideBy4”
         is the incoming signal directly routed to the sensor, where
         each pulse triggers a new quarter period.

   -     If a trigger signal arrives "too early", i.e., when
         the sensor is still busy, the impulse is missed and an erroneous 
         shift by a quarter period introduced. To minimize the chances
         of that happening, the explicit non-sensitive portion of the sensor's
         quarter period schedule is completely scrapped. After the exposed 
         part, :math:`t_{s}`, (configurable by the **Sensitivity** feature
         :math:`p_{s}`) the sensor is directly receptive to the next quarter 
         period trigger. The exposed piece is thus not centered with respect
         to the quarter period as with internal reference triggering, but 
         comes right after the trigger.
         The **Expected Frequency Deviation**, :math:`\mathbf{d}`, gives an
         additional security margin that is necessary should the reference trigger’s
         frequency be unsteady and :math:`p_{s}` be close to one.  It is accounted for in the computation of the fixed
         duration of sensor activity per quarter period :math:`t'_{qp}`.
         The minimal effective quarter period duration, :math:`t^{min}_{qp}`
         is hence the same as :math:`t'_{qp}` and :math:`t_{s}`.

     .. math::
          :label: t_qp_external_reference

          t^{min}_{qp}=t'_{qp}=t_{s}= \frac{p_{s}}{ 4 \cdot \mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}} \cdot \left( 1+\mathbf{d}/100\%\right)}\ \ \ \ \ (\mathbf{external}\ reference)

     .. note::
          Increased trigger timing error tolerance comes at the cost of a reduced
          signal amplitude and **Expected Frequency Deviation** is ignored 
          in the internal reference mode.

.. raw:: latex

    \newpage

.. note::

     The heliSens\ :sup:`TM` S4 has a clock frequency of 80 MHz :math:`\Rightarrow` clock 
     cycle, :math:`t_c` = 12.5 ns. Thus, actually feasible **Reference Frequency** 
     values, :math:`\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}`, are inverse multiples of :math:`t_c\ \times\ 4` - to account for all quarter periods.

     .. math::
          :label: actual_f_ref

          \mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}} = \frac{1}{n\cdot 4 \cdot t_c},\ n\in\left\{146,\ ...,2^{16}\right\}
          \Rightarrow \Delta\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}} = 
          \frac{\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}}{ 4 \cdot t_c \cdot {\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}}^{-1}}-\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}
          \approx 4 \cdot tc \cdot {\mathbf{f'}_{\mathbf{ref}}}^{2}

     In **Internal** reference mode, the effective reference frequency, :math:`f_{ref}`, 
     is restricted to these values. With an **External** reference, other 
     frequencies may be fed in, but individual trigger pulses are registered 
     only with a time resolution of :math:`\pm t_c`. This can produce a 
     period-to-period jitter of the effective :math:`f_{ref}`.
